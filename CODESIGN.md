# Co-design kit — running the session and writing up the evidence

The hackathon's one hard requirement: *"every project must involve at least one real
neurodivergent user in its design or testing"*, and the submission must *"demonstrate,
with evidence"* that it happened. Everything needed to run that is in this file.

Budget **35–45 minutes** with one person. Two people is better than one; three is
plenty. Do it early enough that their feedback can actually change the build.

---

## 1. Who counts, and who to ask

**Who counts:** anyone neurodivergent — ADHD, autistic, dyslexic, dyspraxic,
dyscalculic, Tourette's, OCD. **Formally diagnosed or self-identifying both count.**
Self-identification is standard in neurodiversity spaces precisely because assessment is
gated by cost, access and family; requiring a diagnosis would exclude a large part of the
community, including people in exactly your situation.

They do not have to be a stranger, a student, or an expert. A friend, a sibling, a
classmate is fine.

> **Can I be my own neurodivergent user?** For the **Neurodivergent Innovator Award**
> ("led by a neurodivergent individual"), yes — self-identification covers it. For **this
> requirement**, no, don't rely on it. The rule asks you to *"describe who participated"*
> and how *"feedback shaped the project"*, and the organizers' own Resources page spells
> out the intent: *"The strongest projects don't guess at these needs. They ask a real
> neurodivergent user and build from the answer."* It is a hard requirement, not a scored
> criterion — failing it invalidates the entry rather than costing points.

**Where to look, in order of how fast it will work:**

1. **The IncludAI Discord — https://discord.gg/bEXbrJxvu.** By far the best lead, and the
   organizers point at it for exactly this ("Need a teammate or a mentor? Our Discord
   community is here all week"). This is a neurodiversity hackathon: a large share of the
   159 participants are themselves neurodivergent, they understand the ask immediately,
   and swapping testing with another team is normal. Offer to test theirs in return.

   Message to send:

   > Hey — solo builder here. I've made a learning tool that turns any topic into a 3D
   > model you can pull apart, instead of a wall of text, with dyslexia font, full
   > recolouring, a line reader, TTS and now full keyboard control. It's live and takes
   > about 5 minutes to try: https://diverselearning.vercel.app
   >
   > Looking for a neurodivergent tester for 30 minutes this week — I want to watch
   > someone use it cold and tell me what's annoying. No prep, and you don't have to tell
   > me anything personal. **Happy to test your project back.**

2. **The Devpost discussion board — https://includai-2026.devpost.com/forum_topics.**
   As of Aug 1 it is completely empty, so a post there is the only thing on the board and
   every participant sees it. Suggested title: *"Swapping 30-min user tests? I'll test
   yours if you test mine"* — a swap thread helps everyone hit the same hard requirement,
   which makes it a genuinely useful post rather than a plea.
3. **Email the organizers — contact@includedu.org.** They run a neurodiversity
   non-profit and offer mentors all week. Ask two things in one message: whether they can
   point you to a tester, and (in writing) whether a self-identifying neurodivergent
   solo builder satisfies the requirement on their own. Their answer settles it either
   way, and asking is not a bad look — it shows you read the rules.
4. **People you already know.** Someone in your circle is neurodivergent. You do not
   need to know who in advance — see the script below, which lets people self-select
   instead of being asked a personal question.
5. **ICS Bangkok learning support.** The learning-support or student-services staff work
   with neurodivergent students daily and can pass on a request. Ask the staff member,
   not the students — let them do the introducing.
6. **Online communities.** r/neurodiversity, r/ADHD, r/dyslexia, r/autism, and
   subject Discords. **Read the rules first** — many of these ban recruitment and
   research posts outright, and getting this wrong reflects badly on the project. Where
   it is allowed, post as a student showing a tool, not as a researcher collecting data.

**How to ask without making it weird.** Never ask "are you autistic / do you have ADHD?"
Describe the project and let people opt in:

> I built a learning tool for people who don't get on with text-heavy lessons — it turns
> concepts into 3D models you can pull apart. It's for a neurodiversity hackathon, and I
> need feedback from people who learn differently. Would you be up for 30 minutes of
> clicking around and telling me what's annoying? No prep needed, and you don't have to
> tell me anything personal.

**If your tester is under 18**, get a parent or guardian's OK first, the same as you need
for yourself.

## 2. Consent — read this out before you start

> Thanks for doing this. Three things before we start.
>
> One: I'm testing the software, not you. If something is confusing, that's a bug I need
> to find — it is never you getting it wrong.
>
> Two: you don't have to tell me anything about yourself, any diagnosis, or anything
> personal. When I write this up I'll use your first name only, or "Participant A" if
> you'd rather.
>
> Three: you can stop, skip anything, or take a break at any point, and you don't have to
> give a reason.
>
> The most useful thing you can do is say what you're thinking out loud, including the
> boring or negative bits. "This is annoying" is the single most useful sentence you can
> give me.

Ask before recording anything. Written notes are enough — you do not need audio or video.

## 3. The tasks

Hand them the live site (https://diverselearning.vercel.app) **with no explanation** and
watch. Say as little as possible. When they get stuck, count to ten before helping — the
stuck moments are the findings.

| # | Task | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Open something you'd want to learn about." | Do they find `/learn`? Do they know what to click first? |
| 2 | "Read this lesson." | Where do their eyes go — model or text first? Do they read it all? Do they scroll past anything? |
| 3 | "Find out what one piece of that model does." | Do they discover **Break apart** on their own? Do they know parts are clickable? |
| 4 | "Change how this looks so it's easier for you to read." | Do they find the Appearance menu? Which setting do they reach for first? Do they keep it? |
| 5 | "Try it without the mouse." | New keyboard support — does the legend make sense? Do the keys do what they expect? |
| 6 | "Make a course about something you actually care about." | Is the wait tolerable? Is the result any good? |
| 7 | Show them the **Start here** step card. | Does one-step-at-a-time help, or is it patronising? Ask directly. |

**Questions to close with** (ask exactly these, do not lead):

- What was the most annoying part?
- Was there anywhere you expected something to happen and it didn't?
- Would you use this instead of reading a textbook page? Why, or why not?
- If you could change one thing, what?
- Is there anything about how you learn that this completely misses?

## 5. Turning it into submission evidence

Fix at least two of the things they raised **during the build week**, and commit them
with a message that names the session. That commit history is the evidence — it shows
feedback actually changed the product, which is exactly what the rules ask for and what
the 30%-weighted impact criterion is scored on.

Then fill this in and copy it into the Devpost description:

| Date | Participant | What they tested | What they said | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | *(commit link)* |

**Write-up paragraph** — fill the blanks, keep it concrete:

> DiverseLearning was tested with ___ neurodivergent ___ on ___. They worked through the
> concept library, a lesson, the break-apart model view and the accessibility settings
> with no guidance from me. The clearest finding was ___ — in their words, "___". I also
> learned ___. As a direct result I changed ___ and ___ during the hackathon week
> (commits ___). The thing I got most wrong was ___, which I had assumed ___.

**Do not** write this paragraph before the session happens. An invented co-design story
is the one failure mode that would sink the submission outright, and "we consulted
neurodivergent users" with no names, quotes or resulting changes is exactly what a
neurodiversity-focused judging panel is primed to spot.
